# CS 606T — Advanced Computer Networks

## Queuing Theory — Topic 2: Bernoulli, Binomial & Poisson Random Variables

These three are **very important** because they form the foundation for understanding **Poisson traffic arrivals** and eventually the **M/M/1 queue**.

---

# 1. Bernoulli Random Variable

A Bernoulli experiment has exactly **two possible outcomes**:

* Success
* Failure

Define:

$$
X=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{Success}\\
0 & \text{Failure}
\end{cases}
$$

If probability of success is \(p\), then:

$$
P(X=1)=p
$$

and

$$
P(X=0)=1-p
$$

### Example — Packet transmission

Suppose a packet is successfully transmitted with probability \(0.9\).

Define:

$$
X=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{packet successfully transmitted}\\
0 & \text{packet lost}
\end{cases}
$$

Then:

$$
P(X=1)=0.9
$$

$$
P(X=0)=0.1
$$

This is a **Bernoulli random variable**.

---

# 2. Mean and Variance of Bernoulli RV

For:

$$
X\sim Bernoulli(p)
$$

Mean:

$$
\boxed{E[X]=p}
$$

Variance:

$$
\boxed{Var(X)=p(1-p)}
$$

### Example

If:

$$
p=0.8
$$

then:

$$
E[X]=0.8
$$

and:

$$
Var(X)=0.8(0.2)=0.16
$$

---

# 3. Binomial Random Variable

Now suppose we perform the **same Bernoulli experiment \(n\) times**.

Let:

$$
X=\text{number of successes in }n\text{ trials}
$$

Then:

$$
\boxed{X\sim Binomial(n,p)}
$$

The PMF is:

$$
\boxed{
P(X=k)=
{n\choose k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
}
$$

where:

$$
k=0,1,2,\ldots,n
$$

---

# 4. Why the Combination?

Suppose we have 5 packet transmissions and want exactly 3 successful transmissions.

There are several possible arrangements:

$$
SSSFF
$$

$$
SSFSF
$$

$$
SSF SF
$$

etc.

The number of ways to select which 3 of the 5 positions are successes is:

$$
{5\choose3}
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(X=3)
=
{5\choose3}p^3(1-p)^2
$$

---

# 5. Networking Numerical — Binomial

Suppose a network sends **10 packets**.

Each packet has probability \(0.9\) of being successfully delivered.

What is the probability that **exactly 8 packets** are delivered successfully?

Here:

$$
n=10
$$

$$
p=0.9
$$

$$
k=8
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(X=8)
=
{10\choose8}(0.9)^8(0.1)^2
$$

$$
=45(0.9)^8(0.1)^2
$$

$$
\boxed{P(X=8)\approx0.1937}
$$

So approximately:

$$
\boxed{19.37\%}
$$

---

# 6. Mean and Variance of Binomial

If:

$$
X\sim Binomial(n,p)
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{E[X]=np}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{Var(X)=np(1-p)}
$$

### Example

10 packets, success probability \(0.9\):

$$
E[X]=10(0.9)=9
$$

So on average:

$$
\boxed{9\text{ packets succeed}}
$$

Variance:

$$
Var(X)=10(0.9)(0.1)=0.9
$$

---

# 7. Bernoulli vs Binomial

This is a very common theoretical question.

| Bernoulli        | Binomial           |
| ---------------- | ------------------ |
| One trial        | \(n\) trials       |
| Two outcomes     | Counts successes   |
| \(X=0\) or \(1\) | \(X=0,1,\ldots,n\) |
| Parameter \(p\)  | Parameters \(n,p\) |

You can think of:

$$
\boxed{\text{Bernoulli = one trial}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Binomial = repeated Bernoulli trials}}
$$

---

# 8. Poisson Random Variable

Now we reach the **most important distribution for your networking queueing chapter**.

Suppose we don't care about individual packet success/failure.

Instead, we care about:

> How many packets arrive during a given time interval?

For example:

* packets arriving per second
* customers arriving per minute
* calls arriving per hour
* requests arriving at a server

These can often be modeled using a **Poisson random variable**.

---

# 9. Poisson PMF

If:

$$
X\sim Poisson(\lambda)
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{
P(X=k)=
e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}
}
$$

where:

* \(\lambda\) = average number of events during the interval
* \(k=0,1,2,\ldots\)

---

# 10. Understanding \(\lambda\)

This is extremely important.

Suppose:

> A router receives an average of 100 packets per second.

Then:

$$
\lambda=100
$$

for a **1-second interval**.

For 5 seconds:

$$
\lambda=100\times5
$$

$$
\boxed{\lambda=500}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\lambda=\text{rate}\times\text{time}}
$$

---

# 11. Networking Numerical

Suppose packets arrive at a router according to a Poisson distribution with rate:

$$
\lambda=5\text{ packets/sec}
$$

Find the probability of exactly 3 packets arriving during one second.

We have:

$$
k=3
$$

$$
\lambda=5
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(X=3)
=
e^{-5}\frac{5^3}{3!}
$$

$$
=
e^{-5}\frac{125}{6}
$$

$$
\boxed{P(X=3)\approx0.1404}
$$

So approximately:

$$
\boxed{14.04\%}
$$

---

# 12. Probability of Zero Arrivals

This is especially useful in networking.

Using:

$$
P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}
$$

put:

$$
k=0
$$

Then:

$$
P(X=0)
=
e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^0}{0!}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{P(X=0)=e^{-\lambda}}
$$

### Example

Packets arrive at:

$$
\lambda=4/sec
$$

Probability of no packets arriving during one second:

$$
P(X=0)=e^{-4}
$$

$$
\boxed{\approx0.0183}
$$

So there is approximately a:

$$
\boxed{1.83\%}
$$

chance of no arrivals.

---

# 13. Probability of At Least One Arrival

This is another common numerical.

We want:

$$
P(X\geq1)
$$

Instead of adding:

$$
P(X=1)+P(X=2)+P(X=3)+\cdots
$$

use the complement:

$$
P(X\geq1)=1-P(X=0)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{P(X\geq1)=1-e^{-\lambda}}
$$

For:

$$
\lambda=4
$$

$$
P(X\geq1)=1-e^{-4}
$$

$$
\boxed{\approx0.9817}
$$

---

# 14. Poisson Mean and Variance

For:

$$
X\sim Poisson(\lambda)
$$

we have:

$$
\boxed{E[X]=\lambda}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{Var(X)=\lambda}
$$

This is a very important property.

Unlike Binomial:

$$
Var(X)=np(1-p)
$$

For Poisson:

$$
\boxed{\text{Mean}=\text{Variance}=\lambda}
$$

---

# 15. Poisson vs Binomial

This is a favorite conceptual question.

### Binomial

We have:

* fixed number of trials \(n\)
* each trial has success probability \(p\)
* count successes

$$
P(X=k)=
{n\choose k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
$$

### Poisson

We have:

* events occurring over time/space
* average rate \(\lambda\)
* count events in an interval

$$
P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}
$$

---

# 16. Poisson as an Approximation to Binomial

When:

$$
n\rightarrow\text{large}
$$

and:

$$
p\rightarrow0
$$

while:

$$
np=\lambda
$$

remains finite, Binomial can be approximated by Poisson.

So:

$$
\boxed{Binomial(n,p)\approx Poisson(np)}
$$

### Example

Suppose:

$$
n=1000
$$

$$
p=0.002
$$

Then:

$$
np=1000(0.002)=2
$$

Therefore:

$$
Binomial(1000,0.002)
\approx
Poisson(2)
$$

This approximation is very useful when \(n\) is large and \(p\) is small.

---

# 17. Why Poisson Matters So Much in Networking

Suppose:

$$
\lambda=1000\text{ packets/sec}
$$

A queueing model can treat packet arrivals as:

$$
N(t)\sim Poisson(\lambda t)
$$

For a 10 ms interval:

$$
t=0.01
$$

Therefore:

$$
\lambda t=1000(0.01)=10
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{N(0.01)\sim Poisson(10)}
$$

This tells us the probability distribution of the number of packets arriving during those 10 ms.

This becomes the foundation of the **Poisson process**.

# 18. Three Distributions You Must Know

### Bernoulli

One experiment:

$$
\boxed{X\in\{0,1\}}
$$

### Binomial

Number of successes in \(n\) trials:

$$
\boxed{
P(X=k)=
{n\choose k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
}
$$

### Poisson

Number of events in an interval:

$$
\boxed{
P(X=k)=
e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}
}
$$

# ⭐ Exam-Tricky Questions

### Q1

A router receives packets at an average rate of 200 packets/sec. What is the Poisson parameter for 50 ms?

$$
\lambda t=200(0.05)
$$

$$
\boxed{\lambda=10}
$$

---

### Q2

For a Poisson RV with parameter \(\lambda=5\), what are its mean and variance?

$$
\boxed{E[X]=5}
$$

$$
\boxed{Var(X)=5}
$$

---

### Q3

A Poisson random variable has:

$$
P(X=0)=?
$$

Answer:

$$
\boxed{e^{-\lambda}}
$$

---

### Q4

If packet arrivals are Poisson with rate 50 packets/sec, what is the probability of no packet arriving during 20 ms?

$$
\lambda t=50(0.02)=1
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(X=0)=e^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\approx0.3679}
$$

---

### Q5 — Conceptual

> The number of packets arriving in one second is discrete, but the time between two consecutive packet arrivals is continuous. Explain.

**Answer:** Number of arrivals is a count:

$$
0,1,2,\ldots
$$

so it is discrete. Inter-arrival time can take any nonnegative real value, so it is continuous.

This distinction becomes **very important in the Poisson process**.

# 🔥 One Important Connection

You should remember this chain:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Poisson RV}
\rightarrow
\text{Poisson Process}
}
$$

A Poisson RV tells us:

> **How many events occur during an interval?**

A Poisson process tells us:

> **How events occur over time.**

And then:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Poisson Process}
\rightarrow
\text{Exponential Inter-arrival Time}
}
$$

and eventually:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Poisson Arrivals + Exponential Service}
\rightarrow
M/M/1
}
$$

So the next topic is **Exponential Random Variable + Memoryless Property**, which is particularly important because your PPT explicitly derives the memoryless property and uses it in Markov/queueing models.
