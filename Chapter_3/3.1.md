# 1. What is a Random Variable?

A **random variable (RV)** is a function that assigns a numerical value to every possible outcome of a random experiment.

The important point:

> A random variable is **not itself random**. The outcome of the experiment is random; the RV simply converts that outcome into a number.

### Example 1 — Coin

Experiment:

> Toss a coin.

Sample space:

$$
S=\{H,T\}
$$

Define:

$$
X=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if Head}\\
0 & \text{if Tail}
\end{cases}
$$

So:

| Outcome |  X |
| ------- | -: |
| H       |  1 |
| T       |  0 |

Here, \(X\) is a random variable.

---

# 2. Networking Example

Suppose we observe a router for **1 second**.

Let:

$$
X=\text{number of packets arriving during that second}
$$

Possible values:

$$
X=0,1,2,3,4,\ldots
$$

We don't know beforehand how many packets will arrive.

For example:

* 0 packets
* 1 packet
* 5 packets
* 100 packets

Therefore, \(X\) is a random variable.

This particular RV is **discrete**.

---

# 3. Discrete Random Variable

A random variable is **discrete** if its possible values are **countable**.

Examples:

### Network examples

* Number of packets arriving
* Number of packets in a queue
* Number of active connections
* Number of dropped packets
* Number of users connected to a router
* Number of packets transmitted in a time slot

For example:

$$
X=\text{number of packets in buffer}
$$

Possible values:

$$
0,1,2,3,\ldots
$$

These can be counted.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{X\text{ is discrete}}
$$

---

# 4. Continuous Random Variable

A continuous random variable can take values over a continuous interval.

For example:

$$
T=\text{time until the next packet arrives}
$$

Possible values could be:

$$
0.1,\;0.15,\;0.153,\;0.1537,\;0.15372,\ldots
$$

There are infinitely many possibilities between any two values.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{T\text{ is continuous}}
$$

### Network examples

* Packet transmission time
* Packet propagation delay
* Waiting time
* Service time
* Inter-arrival time
* Queueing delay

---

# 5. Very Important Exam Distinction

| Discrete                  | Continuous              |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Countable values          | Uncountably many values |
| Number of packets         | Packet delay            |
| Number of customers       | Waiting time            |
| Number of connections     | Service time            |
| Number of dropped packets | Inter-arrival time      |
| Uses PMF                  | Uses PDF                |

Remember:

$$
\boxed{\text{Counting} \rightarrow \text{Discrete}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Measuring time/length/etc.} \rightarrow \text{Continuous}}
$$

---

# 6. PMF — Probability Mass Function

For a **discrete random variable**, we use a **PMF**.

PMF tells us:

$$
\boxed{P(X=x)}
$$

That means:

> Probability that \(X\) takes exactly the value \(x\).

### Example

Suppose the number of coffees ordered by a customer is:

|  X | P(X=x) |
| -: | -----: |
|  1 |   0.65 |
|  2 |   0.25 |
|  3 |   0.10 |

Then:

$$
P(X=1)=0.65
$$

$$
P(X=2)=0.25
$$

$$
P(X=3)=0.10
$$

And:

$$
0.65+0.25+0.10=1
$$

Therefore it is a valid PMF.

---

# 7. Conditions for a PMF

For a discrete RV:

### Condition 1

$$
P(X=x)\geq0
$$

Probability cannot be negative.

### Condition 2

$$
\sum_x P(X=x)=1
$$

The probabilities of **all possible outcomes must sum to 1**.

### Example

Suppose:

$$
P(X=0)=0.2
$$

$$
P(X=1)=0.5
$$

$$
P(X=2)=0.3
$$

Then:

$$
0.2+0.5+0.3=1
$$

So this is a valid PMF.

---

# 8. PDF — Probability Density Function

For a **continuous random variable**, we use a **PDF**.

Suppose:

$$
T=\text{packet waiting time}
$$

and \(f(t)\) is its PDF.

For continuous variables:

$$
\boxed{P(T=t)=0}
$$

This is extremely important.

You might think:

> Why is the probability of exactly 3 seconds zero?

Because there are infinitely many possible values around 3:

$$
2.999,\;2.9999,\;3,\;3.0001,\;3.001,\ldots
$$

A single point has zero area.

Instead, we calculate the probability over an **interval**.

$$
P(a\leq T\leq b)
=
\int_a^b f(t)\,dt
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{\text{Probability = Area under PDF}}
$$

---

# 9. PMF vs PDF — Extremely Important

This is a common exam question.

### PMF

For discrete \(X\):

$$
\boxed{P(X=x)}
$$

gives the probability of an exact value.

Example:

$$
P(X=5)=0.2
$$

This is meaningful.

---

### PDF

For continuous \(X\):

$$
\boxed{P(X=x)=0}
$$

Instead:

$$
\boxed{P(a<X<b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx}
$$

---

# 10. Example — Continuous Networking Variable

Suppose packet waiting time \(T\) is continuous.

Question:

> What is the probability that the packet waits between 2 ms and 5 ms?

If the PDF is \(f(t)\):

$$
P(2\leq T\leq5)
=
\int_2^5 f(t)\,dt
$$

You **do not** calculate:

$$
P(T=2)+P(T=5)
$$

That's wrong.

---

# 11. CDF

Another important concept.

The **Cumulative Distribution Function** is:

$$
\boxed{F_X(x)=P(X\leq x)}
$$

It gives the probability that the random variable is less than or equal to \(x\).

### Example

Suppose:

$$
X=\text{number of packets arriving}
$$

and:

$$
P(X=0)=0.2
$$

$$
P(X=1)=0.5
$$

$$
P(X=2)=0.3
$$

Then:

$$
F(1)=P(X\leq1)
$$

Therefore:

$$
F(1)=P(X=0)+P(X=1)
$$

$$
=0.2+0.5
$$

$$
\boxed{F(1)=0.7}
$$

---

# 12. Important Difference: PMF vs CDF

PMF:

$$
P(X=x)
$$

CDF:

$$
P(X\leq x)
$$

Example:

$$
P(X=2)=0.3
$$

but:

$$
P(X\leq2)=1
$$

because:

$$
0.2+0.5+0.3=1
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random Experiment}
\rightarrow
\text{Random Variable}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Discrete}\rightarrow PMF\\

}

$$
\boxed{\text{Packets/customers} \rightarrow \text{Discrete}}

$$
\boxed{\text{Time/delay/service time} \rightarrow \text{Continuous}}
