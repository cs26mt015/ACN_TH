# 1. The Four Main Types of Network Delay

When a packet travels through a network, its total delay can consist of several components.

```text
Source
  │
  ├── Transmission Delay
  │
  ├── Propagation Delay
  │
  ├── Queuing Delay
  │
  └── Processing Delay
  │
Destination
```

The four important delays are:

$$
\boxed{
D_{total}
=
D_{processing}
+
D_{queuing}
+
D_{transmission}
+
D_{propagation}
}
$$

Let's understand each one.

---

# 2. Transmission Delay

Transmission delay is:

> **The time required to push all bits of a packet onto the link.**

Formula:

$$
\boxed{D_{trans}=\frac{L}{R}}
$$

where:

* \(L\) = packet size in bits
* \(R\) = link transmission rate in bits/sec

### Example

Packet:

$$
L=8000\text{ bits}
$$

Link:

$$
R=2Mbps
$$

Then:

$$
D_{trans}
=
\frac{8000}{2\times10^6}
$$

$$
=0.004s
$$

$$
\boxed{4ms}
$$

---

# 3. Propagation Delay

Propagation delay is:

> **The time required for the signal to travel from one end of the link to the other.**

Formula:

$$
\boxed{D_{prop}=\frac{d}{v}}
$$

where:

* \(d\) = physical distance
* \(v\) = propagation speed

Example:

$$
d=1000km
$$

$$
v=200000km/s
$$

Then:

$$
D_{prop}
=
\frac{1000}{200000}
$$

$$
=0.005s
$$

$$
\boxed{5ms}
$$

---

# 4. Transmission vs Propagation

This distinction is extremely important.

Imagine:

```text
Sender                                      Receiver
  A=============================================B
                  1000 km
```

### Transmission delay asks:

> How long does it take to **put the entire packet onto the link?**

Depends on:

$$
\boxed{\text{Packet size + bandwidth}}
$$

### Propagation delay asks:

> Once a bit has entered the link, how long does it take to **travel to the other end?**

Depends on:

$$
\boxed{\text{Distance + propagation speed}}
$$

---

## Example

Suppose:

$$
L=10000\text{ bits}
$$

$$
R=1Mbps
$$

$$
d=1000km
$$

$$
v=200000km/s
$$

Transmission:

$$
D_{trans}
=
\frac{10000}{10^6}
=
10ms
$$

Propagation:

$$
D_{prop}
=
\frac{1000}{200000}
=
5ms
$$

Therefore:

$$
D_{total}=10+5=15ms
$$

assuming no processing or queuing delay.

---

# 5. Queuing Delay

Now imagine multiple packets arrive at a router.

```text
P1 ──┐
P2 ──┤
P3 ──┤──→ Router ──→ Output link
P4 ──┘
```

The outgoing link cannot necessarily transmit all packets simultaneously.

So packets wait in a buffer:

```text
Router buffer:

┌────┐
│ P4 │
├────┤
│ P3 │
├────┤
│ P2 │
├────┤
│ P1 │ → transmission
└────┘
```

The time a packet spends waiting is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Queuing Delay}}
$$

Unlike transmission and propagation delay, queuing delay can be **highly variable**.

It depends on:

* traffic load
* number of packets waiting
* transmission rate
* congestion

---

# 6. Processing Delay

Before forwarding a packet, a router needs to process it.

Conceptually:

```text
Packet arrives
     ↓
Check header
     ↓
Check destination
     ↓
Lookup forwarding table
     ↓
Choose output
     ↓
Forward
```

The time spent performing these operations is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Processing Delay}}
$$

It can include:

* examining packet headers
* checking errors
* looking up forwarding information
* determining outgoing interface

---

# 7. Total Delay

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
D_{total}
=
D_{proc}
+
D_{queue}
+
D_{trans}
+
D_{prop}
}
$$

This formula is extremely important.

---

# 8. Multiple Hops

Now consider:

```text
A → R1 → R2 → B
```

There are **3 links/hops**:

```text
A → R1
R1 → R2
R2 → B
```

Suppose every link has:

$$
R=2Mbps
$$

and every packet is:

$$
L=2000bits
$$

Transmission delay per link:

$$
D_{trans}
=
\frac{2000}{2\times10^6}
$$

$$
=1ms
$$

For a **single packet**, if propagation, processing, and queuing delays are ignored:

```text
A → R1       1 ms
R1 → R2      1 ms
R2 → B       1 ms
```

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{3ms}
$$

for one packet.

---

# 9. Multiple Packets — Pipeline Effect

Now things become more interesting.

Suppose we have:

$$
P=3
$$

packets:

```text
P1
P2
P3
```

and:

$$
N=3
$$

hops.

Each packet requires:

$$
1ms
$$

transmission time per link.

### Timeline

```text
Time →

Link 1:
P1 | P2 | P3
   1    2    3 ms

Link 2:
   P1 | P2 | P3
   2    3    4 ms

Link 3:
       P1 | P2 | P3
       3    4    5 ms
```

Therefore the final packet arrives at:

$$
\boxed{5ms}
$$

---

# 10. General Formula

If:

* \(P\) = number of packets
* \(N\) = number of hops
* \(T\) = transmission time per packet per link

and propagation/queuing/processing delays are ignored:

$$
\boxed{
D=(P+N-1)T
}
$$

Since:

$$
T=\frac{L}{R}
$$

we get:

$$
\boxed{
D=(P+N-1)\frac{L}{R}
}
$$

This is exactly the formula used in your professor's packet-switching numerical.

---

# 11. Why Isn't It \(P\times N\times T\)?

This is a common exam trap.

Someone might calculate:

$$
3\times3\times1=9ms
$$

But that's incorrect under the pipeline model.

Why?

Because while P1 is traveling through hop 2, P2 can already be transmitted over hop 1.

```text
Time →

Hop 1: P1 | P2 | P3
Hop 2:    P1 | P2 | P3
Hop 3:        P1 | P2 | P3
```

Multiple packets are **pipelined** through the network.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{5ms}
$$

not 9 ms.

---

# 12. Important Comparison

### One packet

$$
D=N\times T
$$

### Multiple packets

$$
D=(P+N-1)T
$$

assuming:

* equal link transmission rates
* equal packet sizes
* store-and-forward
* negligible propagation
* negligible processing
* negligible queuing

---

# 13. Numerical Example

Suppose:

* 5 packets
* 4 hops
* each packet = 1000 bits
* link rate = 1 Mbps

Then:

$$
T=\frac{1000}{10^6}
$$

$$
T=1ms
$$

Total:

$$
D=(5+4-1)(1ms)
$$

$$
D=8ms
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{8ms}
$$

---

# 14. What Happens If Propagation Delay Is Included?

Now suppose every link additionally has:

$$
D_{prop}=2ms
$$

For the previous example:

$$
P=5
$$

$$
N=4
$$

$$
D_{trans}=1ms
$$

A simplified pipeline model gives:

$$
D=(P+N-1)D_{trans}+N D_{prop}
$$

Therefore:

$$
D=(5+4-1)(1)+4(2)
$$

$$
=8+8
$$

$$
\boxed{16ms}
$$

Again, the exact formula depends on the assumptions of the problem, but this is the standard simplified model for identical links.

# 15. Circuit Switching vs Packet Switching: Delay Perspective

### Circuit switching

There is an initial:

$$
\boxed{Setup\ Delay}
$$

Then data transmission occurs over the established circuit.

Conceptually:

```text
Setup → Data → Teardown
```

### Datagram switching

No setup:

```text
Packet → Forward → Packet → Forward
```

But packets can experience:

* processing delay
* queuing delay
* transmission delay
* propagation delay

So the tradeoff is roughly:

```text
Circuit:
Initial setup
     ↓
More predictable communication

Packet:
No setup
     ↓
Potentially variable delay
```

---

# 16. Important Exam Concept: Bandwidth vs Delay

Suppose bandwidth increases:

$$
10Mbps \rightarrow 100Mbps
$$

What happens?

Transmission delay:

$$
D_{trans}=\frac{L}{R}
$$

decreases.

But propagation delay:

$$
D_{prop}=\frac{d}{v}
$$

doesn't change merely because bandwidth increased.

Therefore:

> **Increasing bandwidth reduces transmission delay but does not inherently reduce propagation delay.**

---

# 17. Another Important Concept: Distance vs Bandwidth

Suppose distance doubles:

$$
d\rightarrow2d
$$

Then:

$$
D_{prop}\rightarrow2D_{prop}
$$

But transmission delay remains unchanged if packet size and bandwidth remain unchanged.

# CS 606T — Delay Analysis: Answers

### Q1. A 4000-bit packet is transmitted over a 4 Mbps link. Find transmission delay.

Formula:

$$
D_{trans}=\frac{L}{R}
$$

Given:

$$
L=4000\ bits
$$

$$
R=4Mbps=4\times10^6\ bits/s
$$

Therefore:

$$
D_{trans}
=
\frac{4000}{4\times10^6}
$$

$$
=0.001s
$$

$$
\boxed{D_{trans}=1ms}
$$

---

### Q2. A signal travels 1500 km at \(2\times10^5\) km/s. Find propagation delay.

Formula:

$$
D_{prop}=\frac{d}{v}
$$

Given:

$$
d=1500km
$$

$$
v=2\times10^5km/s
$$

Therefore:

$$
D_{prop}
=
\frac{1500}{200000}
$$

$$
=0.0075s
$$

Converting to milliseconds:

$$
0.0075\times1000=7.5ms
$$

$$
\boxed{D_{prop}=7.5ms}
$$

---

### Q3. A packet travels through 5 hops. Each link has transmission delay 2 ms. Ignore all other delays. Find total delay for one packet.

There are 5 links/hops.

Each hop requires:

$$
2ms
$$

Therefore:

$$
D=5\times2
$$

$$
\boxed{D=10ms}
$$

### Answer: **10 ms**

---

### Q4. There are 4 packets and 5 hops. Each packet takes 2 ms to transmit on each link. Find the time for the entire message to arrive.

Use the pipeline formula:

$$
D=(P+N-1)T
$$

Given:

$$
P=4
$$

$$
N=5
$$

$$
T=2ms
$$

Therefore:

$$
D=(4+5-1)(2)
$$

$$
=8\times2
$$

$$
\boxed{D=16ms}
$$

### Answer: **16 ms**

### Q5. Why isn't the answer to Q4 \(4\times5\times2=40ms\)?

**Answer:**

Because the packets can be **pipelined** through the network.

The next packet can begin transmission on an earlier link while the previous packet is being transmitted on a later link.

For example:

```text
Time →

Hop 1: P1 | P2 | P3 | P4
Hop 2:    P1 | P2 | P3 | P4
Hop 3:        P1 | P2 | P3 | P4
Hop 4:            P1 | P2 | P3 | P4
Hop 5:                P1 | P2 | P3 | P4
```

The transmissions overlap.

Therefore, we don't add the transmission time of every packet on every hop sequentially.

Instead:

$$
D=(P+N-1)T
$$

$$
=(4+5-1)(2)
$$

$$
=\boxed{16ms}
$$

---

### Q6. If bandwidth increases from 10 Mbps to 100 Mbps, which delay changes directly?

**Answer:**

$$
\boxed{\text{Transmission delay}}
$$

Because:

$$
D_{trans}=\frac{L}{R}
$$

When \(R\) increases, transmission delay decreases.

For example, for the same packet:

```text
10 Mbps → larger transmission delay
100 Mbps → smaller transmission delay
```

However:

$$
D_{prop}=\frac{d}{v}
$$

does not directly depend on bandwidth.

### Therefore:

> Increasing bandwidth directly reduces transmission delay, not propagation delay.

---

### Q7. If physical distance doubles while bandwidth remains constant, which delay changes?

**Answer:**

$$
\boxed{\text{Propagation delay}}
$$

Because:

$$
D_{prop}=\frac{d}{v}
$$

If:

$$
d\rightarrow2d
$$

then:

$$
D_{prop}\rightarrow2D_{prop}
$$

Transmission delay:

$$
D_{trans}=\frac{L}{R}
$$

remains unchanged because packet size and bandwidth haven't changed.

### Therefore:

> **Propagation delay doubles; transmission delay remains unchanged.**

---

### Q8. A router has a packet waiting in its output buffer because another packet is currently being transmitted. Which delay is responsible?

**Answer:**

$$
\boxed{\text{Queuing delay}}
$$

The packet has already arrived at the router but cannot immediately use the outgoing link.

It must wait:

```text id="gmb2y4"
Packet arrives
      ↓
   [Buffer]
      ↓
   WAITING
      ↓
Outgoing link becomes free
      ↓
Transmission
```

The time spent waiting in the buffer is **queuing delay**.

---

### Q9. A 10 MB file is sent over a 100 Mbps link. If the distance is doubled but bandwidth remains unchanged, does transmission time change?

**Answer:**

$$
\boxed{\text{No}}
$$

Transmission delay is:

$$
D_{trans}=\frac{L}{R}
$$

Neither:

* \(L\) = file size
* \(R\) = bandwidth

has changed.

Therefore transmission time remains the same.

However, propagation delay **does increase** because:

$$
D_{prop}=\frac{d}{v}
$$

and distance doubled.

### Important distinction:

> **Distance affects propagation delay, not transmission delay.**

---

### Q10. Why can packet switching have lower resource wastage for bursty traffic but potentially higher and more variable delay than circuit switching?

**Answer:**

### Packet switching

Packet switching uses **shared network resources**.

Suppose:

```text id="m7tq7w"
User A → ███──────████
User B → ──████────────
User C → █────████────
```

When User A is idle, other users can use the network capacity.

Therefore, packet switching is efficient for **bursty traffic**.

However, because multiple users share resources, packets may have to wait in queues:

```text id="4u8q8x"
P1 ──┐
P2 ──┤
P3 ──┤ → Router → Output
P4 ──┘
       ↑
    Queue
```

This creates **queuing delay**, and the amount of delay can vary depending on network load.

---

### Circuit switching

Circuit switching establishes a circuit and reserves resources:

```text id="y9e5n0"
A ═══════════════ B
    Reserved
    resources
```

This can provide more predictable performance after setup.

However, if the user becomes idle:

```text id="xj20hx"
Data:      ███───────████
Resources: ██████████████
```

the reserved resources may remain unused.

Therefore:

> **Circuit switching can waste resources during idle periods, while packet switching shares resources more efficiently but can experience variable queuing delay.**
